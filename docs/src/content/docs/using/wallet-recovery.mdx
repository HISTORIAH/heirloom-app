An heir does not have to be another person. Name a **second wallet you control** and the
same machinery becomes a recovery path for yourself.

## The arrangement

<Steps>
  <Step title="Today">
    The estate answers to your main wallet. Balances sit inside it, earning if you routed
    them. You check in on your schedule and nothing moves.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The bad day">
    Device gone, phrase gone, main wallet unreachable. The check-ins stop — not because
    you chose to stop, but because you cannot sign any more.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The way back">
    The interval lapses, the grace period runs, and your backup wallet claims the estate
    on-chain. No seed phrase was ever shared with anyone, and no third party was ever
    involved.
  </Step>
</Steps>

The cost of that recovery is time — your interval plus your grace period — and the 0.75%
claim fee.

## Setting it up

Identical to any other estate: open one and name your backup wallet's address as the
heir. What makes it recovery rather than inheritance is only who holds that second key.

<Callout type="tip" title="Keep the two apart">
The whole point is that one failure cannot take both. Different device, different seed
phrase, different physical location. A backup wallet stored beside the main one recovers
you from a lost key and not from a lost bag.
</Callout>

## Tuning the clock for recovery

Recovery pulls the trade-off the opposite way from inheritance.

- **Shorter is better here.** The interval is how long you are locked out before the
  recovery path opens. A 30-day interval plus 7-day grace means a five-week wait after a
  bad day.
- **But not too short.** The same window is how quickly the backup wallet gains the power
  to take everything — if that key is stolen, the thief only has to wait.

A common shape is a short interval with a longer grace period: you notice a missed
check-in quickly, but there is real time to recover the main wallet before the backup can
act.

## What this does not protect against

- **Both keys lost.** There is no third path. Heirloom moves entitlement from one key to
  another; it never removes the requirement for a key.
- **A compromised backup.** Whoever holds the backup key can claim the estate once the
  window passes. Treat it exactly as seriously as the main one.
- **Assets outside the estate.** Only what is in the vault is recoverable. Balances left
  in the main wallet go with the key.

## Recovery and inheritance in one estate

They do not combine — an estate has exactly one heir. If you want both, open two: one
naming your backup wallet with a short clock, one naming a person with a long one, and
fund each with what belongs there. They run independently.
