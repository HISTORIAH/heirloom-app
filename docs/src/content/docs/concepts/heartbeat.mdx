A heartbeat is a signed transaction that writes the current time into `last_heartbeat`.
That is the whole mechanism. It carries no amount, moves nothing, and changes no setting
unless you asked it to.

## The deadline

Two durations, both yours, both capped at 365 days:

| Field | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `heartbeat_interval` | How long you may go quiet before the estate is considered at risk |
| `grace_period` | Extra time after that, before the heir can claim |

The program adds them to your last check-in:

<Code caption="programs/heirloom/src/instructions/claim.rs">
```rust
let claimable_at = estate.last_heartbeat
    + estate.heartbeat_interval
    + estate.grace_period;

require!(now >= claimable_at.max(estate.paused_until), NotYetClaimable);
```
</Code>

So a 30-day interval with a 7-day grace gives an heir nothing to do until **37 days**
after your last signature.

## Why two numbers instead of one

They mean different things to the person watching the dashboard. The interval is your
normal cadence — miss it and you are late, not gone. The grace period is the alarm: the
estate is visibly at risk, the app is warning you, and a guardian can still buy you more
time. Only after both have run does anything become possible for the heir.

Collapsing them into one number would work mechanically and would remove the state where
you can be warned before anything is at stake.

## What resets the clock

Any successful `update_field` instruction, from either the authority **or** the registered
hot signer. The app calls this "Send heartbeat" when it passes no settings at all.

```ts
// A heartbeat is an update with nothing to update.
await updateFields(client, signer, {
  heir,
  heartbeatInterval: null,
  gracePeriod: null,
  pauseDuration: null,
  label: null,
});
```

Any *other* estate write — depositing, registering an asset, routing yield — does not
reset it. Only `update_field` touches `last_heartbeat`.

<Callout type="tip" title="Keep the cold key cold">
Register a `hb_signer`: a hot wallet on your phone that may send check-ins and nothing
else. It cannot change settings, move funds, or reassign the heir. If the signer's
arguments include settings and the signer is not the authority, the program silently
ignores them.
</Callout>

## Checking in while paused

If a guardian has deferred the estate, `paused_until` holds a future timestamp. A
heartbeat during that window updates `last_heartbeat` as normal but leaves `paused_until`
alone — the pause is only cleared by a heartbeat sent **after** it has already expired.

<Code caption="programs/heirloom/src/instructions/update_field.rs">
```rust
estate.last_heartbeat = now;

// Clear deferred state when the authority returns and sends a heartbeat
if estate.paused_until > 0 && now >= estate.paused_until {
    estate.paused_until = 0;
}
```
</Code>

The consequence is worth knowing: while a pause is live it is a *floor* on the claim
time, so the estate is safe regardless. Once it lapses, your next check-in clears the
flag and frees the guardian to defer again should they ever need to.

## Choosing the numbers

There is no right answer, but there is a trade-off with exactly two sides.

- **Short interval** — the estate reaches your heir sooner in the event that matters, and
  you sign more often. Miss a few and you are relying on the grace period.
- **Long interval** — you barely think about it, and an heir waits longer for something
  they may urgently need.

Most people start around **30 days interval / 7 days grace** and adjust after living with
it for a month. Both are editable at any time from the dashboard, and editing them also
counts as a check-in.

<Callout type="warning" title="An interval of zero is legal">
`validate_interval` rejects negative values and anything above 365 days, but zero passes.
An estate with a zero interval and a zero grace is claimable the instant the clock ticks
past your last heartbeat. That is a valid configuration for testing and almost never what
you want in production.
</Callout>
