Every crypto inheritance plan ends with the same question: what do you actually hand your heir? A seed phrase on paper can be found, copied or thrown away. A wallet address means your heir needs a wallet, and needs to still have it years from now.

The Heirloom mobile app gives you another answer. You can name a card as your heir. When the time comes, they tap it on their phone to claim.

If your heir already has a Tangem card, [that works too](/blog/tangem-cards-as-heir-credentials/). This post is about the other option: a blank Heirloom card, ring or band that you set up yourself.

## What the card is

An Heirloom card is an NFC chip with one job: to hold one Solana key and sign with it.

The key is created on the chip the first time you set it up. It can't be read out, copied or exported, and the chip won't create a second one over it. The card only ever shares two things: its public key, which becomes your heir's address on the estate, and signatures.

Cards, rings and bands all use the same chip. Pick whatever your heir is least likely to lose.

## Setting it up

When you create an estate in the app:

1. Under **Who inherits this estate?**, choose **Give them an Heirloom credential**
2. Pick **Heirloom card**
3. Choose how your heir will use it (more on that below)
4. Tap the blank card on your phone

The app creates the key on the chip, then asks the card to sign a test message and checks the signature before it goes any further. The card's address becomes the heir on your estate, and the app sends it 0.02 SOL so it can pay its own fees later. Your heir never has to buy SOL to claim.

## Two ways to use it

You choose this when you set up the card.

### Tap to claim, once

There's nothing for your heir to remember. They tap the card, the estate pays out, and they send the funds on to a wallet or exchange account of their choice. After that the card has done its job.

The trade-off is that whoever holds the card can claim with it. Treat it like a key to a safe deposit box: give it to the person, not to a drawer they share.

### Keep it as their wallet

You set a PIN on the chip. The card won't sign anything without it, and three wrong tries lock it. After claiming, your heir can leave the funds on the card and keep using it as a hardware wallet.

Give them the PIN separately. Not written on the card, and not stored in the app.

## What your heir sees

Until your check-in lapses, nothing. Tapping the card shows no amounts, no owner and no estate. Someone who finds it learns only that it's a card.

Once the grace period ends, tapping it on any phone with the Heirloom app opens the claim. The app checks that the card is the heir on that estate before it builds anything, and checks every signature the card returns against that address before sending it. If they tap the wrong card, the app tells them.

## Not the final form

The cards you can get today are early prototypes, and the way they fit into an estate is still changing. Things like replacing a lost card are being reworked, so expect the details here to move before we lock them down. We'll write up what changes when it does.
